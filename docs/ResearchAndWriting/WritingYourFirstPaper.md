# Writing Your First Paper

## Planning

- Agree with the PI on the paper's **main message**, target journal, and author list early.
- Outline first: key figures → results → methods → introduction → abstract.
- Build a timeline (a simple Gantt chart helps) and share drafts early and often.

## Write the bullet points the moment you make a figure

```{admonition} The lab's #1 writing habit
:class: tip
Whenever you create an insightful figure, **immediately write 5–6 bullet points** about it,
ordered **from most important to least important**:

1. **The main finding**: what the figure shows, in one sentence.
2. **Additional important information** that supports or qualifies the main finding.
3. **Useful details** worth pointing out (regional exceptions, outliers, caveats, comparisons).
```

**Why right away?** While you look at a figure over and over, adjusting colors, labels, and
layout, its main message becomes so obvious *to you* that it stops feeling important or insightful
enough to say. That's exactly the message the reader needs. Writing it down the moment you first
understand the figure captures it before it fades.

**Why it pays off:**

- Ordering the bullets from most to least important makes you decide what the figure is really
  about.
- Read together, the **first bullets of all your figures tell the story of the paper**, which
  makes it much easier to settle the main message, the figure order, and the title.
- The bullets become the **first draft of the Results section**: each figure's bullets turn into
  a paragraph that opens with the main finding and then adds supporting details.
- They also give you the figure caption and talking points for lab meetings and conference talks.

Keep the bullets next to the figure, for example in the notebook or script that makes it, in your
research notes, or in your 1:1 meeting-notes Google Doc in your Box folder, and bring them to
your 1:1 meetings.

## Target journals

Journals the lab usually publishes in:

| Publisher / area | Journals |
|------------------|----------|
| **AGU** | *Journal of Advances in Modeling Earth Systems (JAMES)*, *Earth's Future*, *Geophysical Research Letters (GRL)*, *JGR: Biogeosciences*, *JGR: Atmospheres* |
| **Copernicus (EGU)**, open access | *Biogeosciences*, *Geoscientific Model Development (GMD)*, *Earth System Dynamics (ESD)* |
| **Global change** | *Global Change Biology*, *Environmental Research Letters (ERL)* |
| **Agriculture and crop modeling** | *Agricultural and Forest Meteorology*, *Field Crops Research*, *Agricultural Systems* |

Choose the journal with the PI early, since it shapes the paper's length, format, and framing.

## Writing workflow

1. Draft in **Overleaf**, the lab's standard, using the journal's LaTeX template, and share the
   project with the PI from the start (see [LaTeX and Overleaf](LaTeXandOverleaf.md)).
2. Generate every figure from scripts in the project repository.
3. Circulate to co-authors with a clear deadline for comments.

## Pre-submission checklist

- [ ] Manuscript follows the journal's format and word/figure limits.
- [ ] Figures are high resolution, colorblind-friendly, and in the required format.
- [ ] Supporting Information is complete.
- [ ] Code repository is cleaned up and archived on Zenodo with a DOI
      ([Project Repositories](../CodingAndData/PaperRepositories.md)).
- [ ] Data availability statement points to public data with DOIs.
- [ ] Author contributions (CRediT) and acknowledgments, including **funding grant numbers**.
- [ ] Cover letter.
- [ ] Suggested reviewers (and any to exclude), agreed with the PI.
- [ ] All co-authors have approved the final version.

## Peer review

Most papers come back with **major or minor revisions**; that is normal. When responding:

- Write a point-by-point **response to reviewers**: quote each comment, then your reply and what
  changed (with line numbers).
- Provide a tracked-changes version of the manuscript.
- Be polite and constructive, even when you disagree.
